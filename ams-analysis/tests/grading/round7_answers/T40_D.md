No. A constant positron-to-antiproton ratio is consistent with a common origin, but it does not prove one.

**What was measured.** [Documented, C77, S01 Phys. Rept. review, Section 6] Above 60 GeV the positron-to-antiproton flux ratio is consistent with a constant. A constant fit over 60-525 GeV gives 2.00 ± 0.035 (stat.) ± 0.06 (syst.), with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12. The antiproton sample in that review is 5.6e5 events over 1-525 GV (C74). The first antiproton paper reports nearly identical rigidity dependence of the antiproton, proton and positron fluxes at about 60-500 GV [Documented, C06, abstract-level]. That is a different variable and range, so do not mix the two.

**What the collaboration said.** [Documented, C77] The review reads this as nearly identical energy dependence at high energies and "suggests a possible common source". It also says continued data taking is needed to confirm the origin. [Documented, C75] Its interpretation of the antiproton data is likewise qualitative, and the review says modelling uncertainties must improve before a definitive interpretation. These are the collaboration's readings, not measurements.

**Why it does not prove a common origin.**
- A constant-ratio fit is a statement about spectral shape over a limited range (60-525 GeV). It says nothing directly about where the particles come from. A chi2/d.o.f. of 7.2/12 shows the constant is not excluded, not that it is the true description. [General method] Agreement within errors is not validation. A shape difference smaller than the stat. + syst. precision (about 3.5% stat. and 3% syst. on the ratio) would be invisible.
- Different sources or mechanisms can give similar spectral shapes. Primary and secondary processes can mimic each other over a limited range, and different production and propagation chains can give similar rigidity or energy dependence. Rule out these alternatives with model comparison, not by the ratio alone.
- The ratio compares positron flux in energy with antiproton flux in energy (or rigidity in the first paper). The two species have different propagation and energy-loss histories, so a flat ratio in one variable is not automatically a flat ratio in another. Positrons lose energy to synchrotron and inverse-Compton processes, antiprotons essentially do not. Neither the ratio nor its constancy is a model-independent origin test.
- Ratios do not cancel acceptance, efficiency or systematics automatically. Classify each nuisance (shared proton contamination, charge confusion, energy scale versus rigidity scale) as correlated, partial or independent, and verify it. The quoted systematic of ±0.06 is the measurement's own, not an allowance for model dependence.
- The range is finite (60-525 GeV). The test of the "common source" idea is where and whether the ratio changes outside or at the edge of that range. [Documented, C77] The review itself notes that a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum is expected if high-energy antiprotons come from dark-matter annihilation, and that none is claimed as observed. The review also states that antiprotons are not produced by pulsars. These are conditional interpretation statements, not results.

**What would strengthen or weaken the claim.** [Proposal] A common-origin hypothesis should be put as a model with a stated prediction. Examples: the predicted ratio and its energy dependence, and a cutoff position. Then fit the combined (positron, antiproton) data with the joint covariance, because they share detector systematics, and test whether a constant is preferred over alternatives with a stated metric and trigger. More statistics at higher energy, and consistency with the electron and proton data (C75: all AMS data should be used to constrain models), would be the discriminators. Secondary-production and propagation uncertainties must be varied as nuisance parameters.

**Bottom line.** The constant ratio is a striking, documented feature that motivates a common-source hypothesis. The collaboration itself words it as "suggests a possible common source" and says confirmation requires more data and better models. It is not a proof.
