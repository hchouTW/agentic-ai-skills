Yes, with a caveat about what the number actually is.

**[Documented, C91, S01 — AMS Phys. Rept. review, "AMS on the ISS Part II," Section 12, full text]** AMS's nitrogen analysis (3.9 million N nuclei, 2.2 GV–3.3 TV) fits the nitrogen flux over its whole rigidity range as a weighted sum of a primary reference (oxygen) and a secondary reference (boron):

Φ_N = Φ_NP + Φ_NS, with Φ_NP = (0.092 ± 0.002) × Φ_O and Φ_NS = (0.61 ± 0.02) × Φ_B (χ²/d.o.f. = 59/64).

The review itself labels the fitted coefficient **0.092 ± 0.002 as "the N/O abundance ratio at the source,"** and compares it to the Solar-System value 0.135 (+0.051, −0.047), calling the two consistent. So you can quote it that way and attribute the framing to AMS — it is not an interpretation you'd be adding on top of the measurement.

**The caveat to carry along:** this "source" abundance is conditional, not a propagation-free measurement in a strict sense. The decomposition assumes oxygen's rigidity dependence is purely primary-like and boron's is purely secondary-like (the review notes O has the lowest secondary content among He/C/O and that B is mostly made from primary C and O — but neither is exactly zero-contamination). The goodness of fit (59/64) tests whether the two-template shape fits the nitrogen data, not whether that primary/secondary assumption itself is correct. So if you quote 0.092 as "the N/O abundance at the source," it's best practice to note in the same breath that it comes from this two-component fit and rests on that assumption — otherwise a reader may take it as a direct, assumption-free abundance measurement.

[Result-interpretation note, general method] Separately: even a well-supported source abundance ratio is a description AMS derives under its propagation-model assumptions, not a model-independent Galactic source-abundance measurement in the "before propagation" sense some readers might expect — treat it as AMS's own definition of "source," not a universal one.
