**No. A constant e+/p-bar ratio is consistent with a common origin but does not prove it.**

**What AMS reports.** [Documented, C77] In the AMS Physics Reports review of the first seven years of data, the positron-to-antiproton flux ratio above 60 GeV is consistent with a constant. A constant fit over 60-525 GeV gives 2.00 ± 0.035 (stat.) ± 0.06 (syst.), with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12. The review reads this as positrons and antiprotons having nearly identical energy dependence at high energy, and says it "suggests" a possible common source. It also says continued data taking is needed to confirm the origin. [Documented, C06] The earlier antiproton paper separately found that the p-bar, proton and positron fluxes have nearly identical rigidity dependence at about 60-500 GV. That is a different paper, variable and range.

**Why this is not proof.**
1. **A fit is not an origin.** [General method] A chi2/d.o.f. of 7.2/12 means only that a constant is not rejected within the stated errors. Many different mechanisms can give a ratio that is flat to within ±3% (stat.) ± 3% (syst.) over this range.
2. **Different sources can mimic a constant ratio.** [General method] Two different production processes could have similar spectral shapes. Secondary production in the interstellar medium is one candidate, and it is the standard expectation for antiprotons. Propagation could also shape the spectra similarly. A constant ratio constrains the spectral shapes. It does not identify a source.
3. **The result is conditional on the model.** [Documented, C77] The review states that antiprotons are not produced by pulsars, and that a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum would be expected if high-energy antiprotons come from dark-matter annihilation. Those are interpretation statements, not measurements. No such cutoff is claimed as observed. [Documented, C75] The review also says modelling uncertainties must improve before a definitive interpretation. It notes that one model agrees qualitatively with the data both with and without a dark-matter contribution. These are collaboration statements and are not evidence for dark matter.
4. **Ratio caveat.** [General method] Acceptance, efficiency and systematic uncertainties do not cancel automatically in a ratio. The quoted systematic (±0.06) comes from a combination whose correlations between the positron and antiproton measurements should be checked. Also, a ±3% (syst.) band can hide a small, slow trend in the ratio.
5. **Statistics and range.** The fit covers 60-525 GeV only. A constant over a limited range does not exclude a departure at higher energy. Both spectra, and especially the antiproton one, have low statistics at the top end. A cutoff, a change of slope or an upturn there would test the common-origin idea.
6. **Variables.** The constant fit [C77] is in energy for the flux ratio. The similar-shape statement [C75] concerns the antiproton-to-proton ratio in rigidity above about 60 GV, and is not this ratio. Keep these separate.

**What would strengthen or weaken the claim.** [Proposal]
- More data at the highest energies to test whether the ratio stays flat or whether the antiproton spectrum shows a cutoff.
- A joint fit of positron, electron, antiproton and proton spectra within a single propagation and source model, with correlated systematics and the statistical test stated in advance. The review itself recommends using all AMS data to constrain models.
- Comparison of the ratio with explicit models, including secondary production with its cross-section and propagation uncertainties, rather than only a flat-line fit.
- Time dependence. The antiproton 11-year result [C07, abstract only] reports smaller time variation than p, e-, e+ and is a separate low-rigidity (1-41.9 GV) result. It does not by itself address the high-energy ratio.

**Bottom line.** The constant ratio is an observation, not an explanation. It motivates a common-source hypothesis, and the collaboration itself calls it suggestive and says more data are needed. It does not prove that positrons and antiprotons share an origin.
