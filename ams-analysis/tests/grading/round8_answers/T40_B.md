No. A constant ratio is consistent with a common origin, but it does not prove one.

**What AMS reports.** [Documented, C77] In the Phys. Rept. review (S01), above 60 GeV the positron-to-antiproton flux ratio is consistent with a constant. A constant fit over 60-525 GeV gives 2.00 ± 0.035 (stat.) ± 0.06 (syst.), with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12. The review reads this as positrons and antiprotons having nearly identical energy dependence at high energies, and says it "suggests" a possible common source. It adds that continued data taking is needed to confirm the origin. The claim ledger itself notes that the constant-ratio result is a fit to that range and does not prove a common origin. [Documented, C06] The earlier antiproton paper (S08) reports nearly identical rigidity dependence of the p̄, p and e⁺ fluxes at about 60-500 GV. That is a different variable and range, and it is abstract-level.

**Why a constant ratio does not prove it.**

1. **A fit is not a test of the hypothesis.** A chi2/d.o.f. of 7.2/12 shows only that a flat line is not rejected within the stated errors, over a limited range. It is not a test against specific alternatives. A ratio that varies slowly or at the few-percent level could be hidden by the combined statistical and systematic uncertainty, and the result says nothing outside 60-525 GeV. Ratios and fractions do not cancel acceptance, efficiency or systematics automatically. The correlated, partial and independent nuisances have to be classified and checked. [General method]
2. **Different sources or mechanisms can give similar spectral shapes.** Two components with different origins, plus propagation that is similar for both species, could produce parallel spectra. A shared shape is necessary for a common source but not sufficient. Inferring the origin is a model step, separate from the measurement. [General method]
3. **Variable and unit caveats.** The review's ratio is in energy (60-525 GeV). The antiproton paper's statement is in rigidity. For singly charged particles with p ≫ m the two are close at high energy, but the conversions must be stated and not assumed. Rigidity, momentum and energy are different variables. [General method]
4. **The review's own interpretation is conditional.** [Documented, C77, C75] The review says antiprotons are not produced by pulsars. It says a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum is expected if high-energy antiprotons come from dark-matter annihilation. It says the ratio and the antiproton spectrum shape are not what is expected if antiprotons come only from cosmic-ray collisions with the interstellar medium. These are the collaboration's qualitative readings, not measurements. The review also says modelling uncertainties (propagation, production cross sections) must improve before a definitive interpretation. In its comparison of nine models, one model agrees qualitatively with the data both with and without a dark-matter contribution. So the data do not discriminate between those scenarios. No cutoff has been observed in the antiproton spectrum.
5. **Related positron result.** [Documented, C35, C97] The positron flux has a source-term cutoff at 4.07 sigma, E_s = 810 (+310, -180) GeV. This is not 5 sigma, and it is a model-dependent fit (a parametrized source term, not a break in the data) without a look-elsewhere correction. It is not evidence of a common origin with antiprotons.

**What would strengthen or weaken the claim.** [Proposal] More statistics at the high-energy end, a shape comparison with an explicit limit on the ratio's slope, a joint treatment of the correlated systematics, and a search for a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum. A significant deviation from a constant, or a cutoff in only one species, would disfavor the common-source reading. A pre-defined metric and a trigger for changing the interpretation are needed. "Reasonable agreement" is not validation. [General method]

**Bottom line.** AMS observes a ratio consistent with a constant of about 2.00 ± 0.07 (stat. and syst. combined in quadrature, my arithmetic) over 60-525 GeV. The collaboration says this suggests a possible common source and does not claim proof. Treat "common origin" as a hypothesis the data are compatible with, not a conclusion.
