No. A constant ratio is consistent with a common origin, but it does not prove one.

**What AMS reported** [Documented, C77; Phys. Rept. 894 review, S01, Section 6]. Above 60 GeV the positron-to-antiproton flux ratio is consistent with a constant. A constant fit over 60-525 GeV gives 2.00 +- 0.035 (stat.) +- 0.06 (syst.), with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12. The review reads this as the two species having nearly identical energy dependence at high energies, and it says this "suggests" a possible common source. It adds that continued data taking is needed to confirm the origin. The collaboration's own wording is therefore "suggests", not "proves". An earlier AMS paper (S08, C06) reported near-identical rigidity dependence of the antiproton, proton and positron fluxes at about 60-500 GV. That is a different variable and range, so it is not the same statement.

**Why a constant ratio does not prove a common origin**
1. **A fit result is not a proof.** The constant fit is a statement about one range (60-525 GeV) and a limited sample. The chi2/d.o.f. of 7.2/12 shows consistency with a constant. It does not exclude a slowly varying ratio that the current errors cannot resolve. Agreement of this kind is not validation. A real test would state a metric and a trigger, for example an allowed slope in the ratio and the uncertainty that would break the constancy claim. [General method]
2. **Different physics can mimic the same shape.** Two species can have the same spectral shape without sharing a source. Both could come from different sources whose spectra happen to be similar in this range. Or both could be shaped mainly by common propagation (diffusion, energy losses, solar modulation, a shared rigidity or energy dependence). A common source and common propagation of different sources produce similar observables. This is a model-level degeneracy that the ratio alone cannot break. [General method]
3. **The variables are not identical.** The positron results are in energy, while the antiproton results are in rigidity (rigidity R = pc/(Ze) in GV, which is not energy). For |Z| = 1 at high energy, rigidity and total energy are close but not the same. A comparison "constant in 60-525 GeV" mixes the two conventions, so any conversion must state Z and mass. [General method]
4. **A ratio does not cancel systematics automatically.** The ratio is formed from two measurements with different selections, different dominant backgrounds (proton contamination and charge confusion for positrons, electrons and charge-confused protons for antiprotons) and different acceptance and efficiency. Shared and independent uncertainties must be classified and propagated; the 0.06 systematic is a result of that treatment, not a guarantee that a small non-constancy is excluded. [General method]
5. **The interpretation is conditional.** In the review's reading, the constant ratio is compatible with a common source, and the review adds conditional statements: antiprotons are not produced by pulsars, and a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum would be expected if high-energy antiprotons come from dark-matter annihilation. These are the collaboration's interpretation (qualitative statements, C75, C77), not measurements. No cutoff is claimed as observed in this range, so the dark-matter scenario is neither confirmed nor excluded here. The review also says that modelling uncertainties (secondary production, propagation) must improve before a definitive interpretation, and that all AMS data (electrons, positrons, protons, nuclei) should be used to constrain models (C75). [Documented, C75]

**What would strengthen or weaken the claim** [Proposal]
- More statistics and a wider range, to test the constant fit with a stated metric (e.g. a fitted slope with its uncertainty and the ratio of exclusion thresholds), plus a check for an antiproton cutoff or a positron cutoff at high energy. The positron flux has a reported source-term cutoff (C04, C97), so a mismatch in the high-energy behaviour would be informative.
- Fitting the positron and antiproton data jointly within source-plus-propagation models, with the joint covariance of shared systematics, rather than treating the ratio as an independent data point.
- Varying the secondary-production and propagation model as a nuisance, because the antiproton secondary background is itself uncertain.

**Bottom line.** AMS measured a ratio consistent with a constant of 2.00 +- 0.035 +- 0.06 over 60-525 GeV (the review's numbers, C77). That is evidence the two spectra have a similar shape, and the collaboration says it suggests a possible common source. It is not a demonstration of a common origin, because propagation effects or different sources with similar spectra can produce the same behaviour, and the interpretation depends on model assumptions that remain uncertain.
