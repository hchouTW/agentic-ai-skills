No. A constant ratio is compatible with a common origin, but it does not prove one.

**What AMS reported** [Documented, C77, S01 Phys. Rept. review, Section 6]: above 60 GeV the positron-to-antiproton flux ratio is consistent with a constant. A constant fit over 60-525 GeV gives 2.00 ± 0.035 (stat.) ± 0.06 (syst.), with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12. The review reads this as positrons and antiprotons having nearly identical energy dependence at high energy, and it "suggests a possible common source". It also says that continued data taking is needed to confirm the origin. This is the collaboration's qualitative interpretation, not a measurement of origin. The earlier antiproton paper reports a related observation in a different variable and range: nearly identical rigidity dependence of the antiproton, proton and positron fluxes at about 60-500 GV [Documented, C06, S08].

**Why it is not proof**

1. **A constant ratio is a statement about spectral shape, not about production.** It shows that the two fluxes have the same energy dependence over 60-525 GeV, within errors. Different mechanisms can produce similar shapes, and a flat ratio over a limited range does not identify the mechanism. The fit's chi2/d.o.f. of 7.2/12 shows consistency with a constant, not exclusion of every non-constant shape. The statistical and systematic errors leave room for a slow trend. [General method]
2. **The result is range-limited.** It covers 60-525 GeV only. Nothing is claimed below 60 GeV, and extrapolating beyond the measured range is not supported. The ratio is also formed from different species with different acceptance, efficiency and charge-confusion systematics. Those systematics do not cancel automatically and need to be classified as correlated, partial or independent (invariant 5). [General method]
3. **The review's own conditional statements show that the question is open** [Documented, C77, C75]:
   - Antiprotons are not produced by pulsars, so a single pulsar-type source for both species is not what the review proposes.
   - If high-energy antiprotons come from dark-matter annihilation, a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum is expected. The review claims no such cutoff as observed.
   - The antiproton-to-proton ratio is described as not expected if antiprotons come only from cosmic-ray collisions with the interstellar medium.
   - The review concludes that modelling uncertainties must improve before a definitive interpretation. One model shown with and without a dark-matter contribution agrees qualitatively with the data in both cases. These are qualitative collaboration statements, not evidence for dark matter.
4. **"Common origin" is underspecified.** It could mean a shared primary source, a shared secondary-production and propagation mechanism, or a shared acceleration site. Positrons also show a spectral feature: a rise from about 25 GeV and a fall above about 284 GeV, with a source-term cutoff reported [Documented, C96, S01]. A model must reproduce those features along with the constant ratio, and also the electron and proton data (C75). Matching one ratio does not constrain all of that.

**What would strengthen or weaken the case** [Proposal]
- More statistics extending the constant-ratio test in energy, ideally with the ratio shown to hold in time and in bins of the shared selection.
- Whether a cutoff or a trend appears in the antiproton spectrum, which the review identifies as the conditional dark-matter signature.
- A joint model fit to the positron, electron, antiproton and proton data with correlated systematics carried through, and with the background (secondary production and propagation) model varied as a nuisance.
- Time dependence: the antiproton time variation over the solar cycle is smaller than that of protons, electrons and positrons [Documented, C07, S09, abstract level]. Any common-origin model would need to explain that as well.

**Bottom line.** A constant positron-to-antiproton ratio of about 2.00 over 60-525 GeV is an observed regularity that motivates the common-source hypothesis. AMS itself words the conclusion as "suggests a possible common source" and does not claim proof. Reaching that conclusion would need model-level discrimination, which the published data do not yet provide. This answer rests on the skill's source index, whose last verification date is 2026-09-20. I have not checked for newer papers.
