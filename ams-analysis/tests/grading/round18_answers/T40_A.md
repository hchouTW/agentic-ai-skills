No. A flat ratio is consistent with a common origin, but it does not prove one.

**What AMS reported (a small correction to the premise).**
- [Documented, C77] This is the positron-to-antiproton flux ratio, in the AMS Physics Reports review (S01). Above 60 GeV the ratio is consistent with a constant.
- A constant fit over 60-525 GeV gives 2.00 +- 0.035 (stat.) +- 0.06 (syst.), with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12.
- The review reads this as antiprotons and positrons having nearly identical energy dependence at high energy, and it says this "suggests a possible common source". It also says continued data taking is needed to confirm the origin.
- Scope: this is a review-level summary, and the numbers are the review's. They rest on about 1.9 million positrons (C95) and 5.6e5 antiprotons (C74).
- Do not confuse it with the antiproton-to-proton similarity (C75, C06), which is in rigidity, a different variable.

**Why it is not proof.**
1. [General method] A constant ratio is an observation about shape. It says nothing directly about mechanism. Two different sources, or two different processes, can give the same energy dependence.
2. A fit with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12 shows only that a constant is not rejected. It does not exclude a slow trend or a modest cutoff. The statistical power is limited at the high-energy end, where the antiproton counts are smallest.
3. Positron and antiproton fluxes are shaped by different physics. Positrons lose energy quickly (synchrotron and inverse Compton losses), so they come from nearby sources. Antiprotons lose almost no energy and propagate over a much larger volume. Equal energy dependence therefore does not imply that the sources, propagation and losses are the same.
4. Alternatives that reproduce a flat ratio:
   - Secondary production by cosmic-ray interactions, including propagation and cross-section uncertainties that are not small.
   - A mixture of components.
   - A dark-matter or pulsar contribution.
   A common-origin claim has to be tested against these through a model-dependent comparison, with the uncertainties propagated.
5. The review adds that antiprotons are not produced by pulsars, and that a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum is expected if high-energy antiprotons come from dark-matter annihilation. A cutoff or trend in the ratio at higher energy would test the idea. More data and further statistics are needed.

**What would change the conclusion.** An extended energy range with higher statistics, a test of whether the constant stays flat, a model comparison including cross-section and propagation uncertainties, and the correlated systematics between the positron and antiproton measurements.

**Summary.** "Consistent with a common source" is a fair description. "Proves a common origin" is not.
