**No. A constant ratio is consistent with a common origin, but it does not prove one.**

**What AMS actually reported** (no web access here, so this rests on the skill's source index, last verified 2026-09-20, and I cannot confirm anything newer)

- [Documented, C77] The positron-to-antiproton flux ratio comes from the AMS Phys. Rept. 894 review (the first seven years of data). Above 60 GeV the ratio is consistent with a constant. A constant fit over 60-525 GeV (in energy) gives 2.00 ± 0.035 (stat.) ± 0.06 (syst.), with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12. The two errors are absolute errors on the value 2.00, not percentages.
- [Documented, C77] The review reads this as the two species having nearly identical energy dependence at high energies, and says it "suggests a possible common source". It also says continued data taking is needed to confirm the origin. That is the collaboration's wording, and it is not a proof.
- [Documented, C95, C74] The samples behind the ratio are the review's: 1.9 million positrons and 5.6e5 antiprotons over 1-525 GV. They are not the earlier antiproton paper's 3.49e5 events up to 450 GV (C06).
- [Documented, C75, C06] The antiproton-to-proton shape similarity above about 60 GV is a different result, in rigidity. The earlier paper's near-identical rigidity dependence of the antiproton, proton and positron fluxes is also in rigidity, in a different paper. Do not mix these variables with the energy-based ratio.

**Why a constant ratio is not proof of a common origin**

1. [General method] Consistency is not exclusion. A fit with chi2/d.o.f. = 7.2/12 says only that a constant is not rejected within the stated errors, over a limited range of 60-525 GeV. The absolute errors are about 1.8% (stat.) and 3% (syst.), so a slowly varying ratio at the few-percent level is not excluded. At the highest energies the statistics are limited.
2. [General method] Different mechanisms can give similar spectral shapes. Two species can share a power-law-like energy dependence from similar propagation and energy-loss behaviour, or from broadly similar secondary production, without a single source. The ratio constrains the energy dependence, not the origin. A constant ratio is a necessary-looking consequence of a common source, not a sufficient condition, so the inference runs the wrong way.
3. [General method] Positrons and antiprotons are different species with different propagation (positrons lose energy to synchrotron and inverse-Compton radiation, antiprotons do not). A constant ratio then needs explaining, and "common origin" is one candidate explanation among several. Whether it is the best one is a model comparison that needs the full spectral and propagation modelling.
4. [Documented, C77, C75] The review's own interpretation is conditional and qualitative. It says antiprotons are not produced by pulsars, and that a cutoff in the antiproton spectrum is expected if high-energy antiprotons come from dark-matter annihilation. No such cutoff is claimed as observed. It also says modelling uncertainties must improve before a definitive interpretation, and that a model shown with and without a dark-matter contribution agrees qualitatively with the data in both cases. These are collaboration statements, not measurements, and they are not evidence for dark matter.
5. [General method] Ratios do not cancel acceptance, efficiency and systematic uncertainties automatically. Each nuisance (template definition, charge confusion, efficiency corrections, unfolding) must be classified as correlated, partial or independent. A claim built on the ratio inherits both analyses' systematics, and the 3% systematic is already dominated by this.

**What would strengthen or weaken a common-source reading**

- [Proposal] A larger data set, extending the energy range, would test whether the ratio stays constant. The relevant observable is a cutoff or deviation at high energy.
- [Proposal] Joint modelling of positrons, electrons, antiprotons, protons and nuclei, with propagation and secondary-production uncertainties as nuisances, would test the common-source hypothesis against alternatives. The review itself says all AMS data should be used to constrain models.

**Bottom line:** a constant positron-to-antiproton ratio of 2.00 ± 0.035 ± 0.06 over 60-525 GeV is a striking observation that is consistent with, and suggestive of, a common source. It is not a proof, and AMS itself does not claim one.
